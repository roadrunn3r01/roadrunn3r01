# Hi, I'm Martina!

I'm Martina Dulanto Ostornol, a third-year Audiovisual Systems Engineering student at Universitat Pompeu Fabra (UPF) in Barcelona.

I thought I'd start by telling you a little about myself, how I ended up studying engineering, and why I'm now learning to build audio software.

## A little backstory

I've always had a burning passion for music. Ever since I was a little kid, I remember being moved by it in ways that sometimes felt almost too intense to explain.

I loved singing and dancing. I took piano lessons for a year, did musical theatre, moved on to guitar lessons, and eventually started taking singing lessons. When I was fourteen, my friends gave me a Roland MIDI keyboard for my birthday. It came with Ableton Live 10 Lite, and together with my first audio interface, it introduced me to the world of music production.

Reading this, you might assume that all those years eventually turned me into a fully accomplished musician. Someone who could play piano and guitar, sing, write songs, produce records, and perform live.

In some ways, I developed those abilities. But the reality is a little more complicated.

I was always passionate about learning new things, but not particularly good at sticking with them. I would become completely absorbed in something, experiment until I hit the first real wall, and then move on to another interest. I wanted to make music, but I didn't always want to learn the rules, practise the scales, or accept the long and sometimes frustrating process of actually mastering an instrument.

So, when the time came to decide what to study, I couldn't confidently say *music*, even though that desire to create was still very much there. If anything, it was growing.

Then my mother came across a degree called Audiovisual Systems Engineering.

I read the programme and knew almost immediately that it was what I wanted to do. I didn't even visit the university. I simply looked at the curriculum and thought, *yeah, that's it.*

There was just one small problem.

I wasn't especially good at maths, I was definitely not good at physics, and I knew very little about programming.

In fact, my strongest subjects in high school were literature and the humanities. Some of my teachers were rather frustrated when I decided to follow a scientific path instead.

Still, I knew it was going to be difficult, and I wanted to try.

## The long way around

Engineering was difficult, but not entirely for the reasons I had expected.

During the first year, we shared much of our curriculum with students in computer engineering, telecommunications, and related degrees. There was mathematics, computer theory, programming, physics, more mathematics, and then some more mathematics.

The content was challenging, of course, but what frustrated me most was how disconnected the effort felt from my actual interests.

To pass my classes, I had to dedicate an enormous amount of time to subjects whose relevance to music I couldn't yet see. And that was time I desperately wanted to spend writing songs, learning instruments, and producing.

I found myself in a rather ridiculous situation: whenever I was making music, I felt guilty for not studying. Whenever I was studying, I felt guilty for not making music.

Eventually, I became so overwhelmed by that contradiction that I sometimes ended up doing neither.

For a while, it felt as though I had chosen a path that was taking me further away from the very thing that had made me choose it.

## Finding the connection

Then, sometime during my second year, something changed.

I discovered sound synthesis.

Now, you might reasonably wonder how someone who had spent so many years obsessing over music could only just have discovered synthesizers.

Well, I knew what a synthesizer *was*. At least, I thought I did. To me, it was essentially a keyboard capable of making all sorts of strange and wonderful sounds.

What I hadn't understood was **how those sounds were actually made**.

And suddenly, everything started connecting.

Fourier analysis, which I had previously encountered in lectures without much enthusiasm, began to make sense in an entirely different context. Digital signal processing, sampling, the Nyquist theorem, signal representations — concepts that had once seemed abstract and disconnected from my interests turned out to be fundamental to almost every tool I was using to make music at home.

The synthesizers, effects, recordings, and digital instruments I had been working with for years were built upon the very principles I was studying at university.

It was a strange kind of redemption.

For the first time, I understood that engineering and music weren't two competing parts of my life. They were deeply connected, and I had simply never learned how to see that connection.

Maybe I hadn't mastered all those guitar scales. Maybe I still had a long way to go with my singing technique. Maybe I wasn't the accomplished musician I had imagined becoming when I was younger.

But I was beginning to discover another kind of possibility.

**I could build my own instruments.**

And not just instruments. I could create effects, experiment with synthesis, design new ways of interacting with sound, and eventually develop an entire ecosystem of tools around the music I wanted to make.

Suddenly, the things I was learning in engineering no longer felt like obstacles standing between me and music. They became another way of approaching it.

## Building my own tools

I'm still trying to figure out what that ecosystem looks like.

I haven't really released my own music yet, and I certainly haven't mastered everything I want to learn. I'm still writing songs, experimenting with production, discovering new sounds, and trying to understand what kind of musician and engineer I want to become.

But I feel like I'm finally building a path that makes sense to me.

The projects you'll find here are the first building blocks of that path.

I'm learning C++, JUCE, digital signal processing, and sound synthesis, and I want to understand these subjects as deeply as I can. I know that will take time, mistakes, experiments, and probably a lot of projects that don't work the first time.

That's why I'm not particularly concerned about the simplicity of my first plugins.

A gain control might not be the most exciting piece of audio software in the world, but there's something important to me about understanding even the simplest operation from the ground up: how a signal is represented, how it is processed, how a parameter affects it, and how all of that becomes a tool someone can actually use.

I hope that, as I keep learning, the projects I share here will become more ambitious, more personal, and increasingly connected to my songwriting and production.

I don't want to learn programming simply to make software, or study sound synthesis just to reproduce instruments that already exist. I want to explore what happens when technical knowledge becomes part of a creative process, and when the tools themselves begin to shape the music.

There's something incredibly beautiful about seeing different parts of your life, things that once seemed completely unrelated, gradually converge into something that feels right for you.

Whether that's divine intervention or simply the way we grow and evolve, I don't know.

But it makes me happy.
